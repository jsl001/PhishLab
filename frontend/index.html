Why it matters

Scams often look like ordinary conversations—until the ask changes.

A friendly introduction can gradually shift toward secrecy, a private channel, money, or sensitive information. Those changes are easy to miss when reviewed one message at a time. PhishLab is a research prototype for exploring how conversation context and persona can affect the way those signals appear.

Prototype, not a proven detector

This demo uses fictional conversations and model-generated assessments. It does not establish scam prevalence, detection accuracy, or real-world effectiveness.

A conversation can change in stages

These are broad awareness stages, not a required script or a claim that every case follows the same path.

01 · ContactUnexpected hello

A wrong-number or online introduction begins an unfamiliar conversation.

02 · RapportFamiliarity grows

Repeated personal conversation may be followed by pressure to move privately.

03 · ShiftA new opportunity or problem

Investment, task, parcel, account, or authority claims appear in the conversation.

04 · RequestPressure to act

Urgency, secrecy, payment, credentials, or account access may be requested.

One cue alone is not proof. Labels in this prototype are model assessments and should be checked against the conversation context.

A research tool for exploring how scam signals appear in conversation.

Conversation-level signal

Multi-Turn Behavioral Scam Classification

A lightweight rule-based review cue that looks across recent conversation context, rather than relying only on isolated keywords.

Rolling Multi-Turn Window

Tracks the latest 5 messages as a conversation window. It uses simple pattern matching, not a trained state machine or single-line verdict.

Playbook Heuristics

Surfaces cues associated with Sha Zhu Pan (pig-butchering): wrong-number pivots, wealth/crypto claims, off-platform or link/app cues, and high-pressure urgency.

Weighted Risk Scoring

Combines fixed cue weights into a 0–100 heuristic score. Scores at or above 50 are flagged for human review—not treated as proof or used to automatically deploy a persona.

Singapore Threat Localization

Localized cues are heuristic patterns for review, not confirmation that an account or message is fraudulent.

CDC & GST voucher lures

Voucher mentions paired with Telegram or other off-platform requests.

Singpass OTP phishing

Requests or claims involving Singpass one-time passwords.

IRAS & bank impersonation

Tax-refund claims and DBS/OCBC fraud-team impersonation cues.

PayNow & PayLah traps

Local payment references and bank OTP or phishing-link language.

The score is not a calibrated confidence percentage. These cues can appear in legitimate conversations; check the message context before drawing conclusions.

How the investigation works

What does PhishLab do?

PhishLab helps researchers review suspicious conversations, generate controlled replies, and organise possible scam indicators for human investigation.

What data does PhishLab collect?
  1. Conversation input: the message text, thread ID, sender role, and timestamp are saved to the configured database. Local development uses SQLite; the hosted deployment uses PostgreSQL.
  2. Model analysis: up to the latest 15 messages are processed by the configured model to generate the selected persona’s reply and structured observations: scam stage, pressure, extracted artifacts, delay strategy, and notes. The model status at the top of the page shows whether processing is local or external.
  3. Research record: the reply, persona/settings, model observations, and extracted artifacts are stored in the app database for this conversation view, analytics, and export.

The guided scenario uses fictional, researcher-selected messages. It does not connect to WhatsApp or send messages externally.

How does PhishLab check links?

Use the Link safety check in the conversation panel to submit a URL to Google Safe Browsing. The full URL, including its path and query, is sent to Google. PhishLab does not open the link, follow redirects, or save the checked URL.

  • Known threat: Google returned a threat-list match; treat it as potentially dangerous.
  • No known threat: no match was returned at check time. This does not guarantee safety.
  • Without an API key, the URL is not sent and the check reports that it is not configured.

Google Safe Browsing is for non-commercial use; commercial deployments should review Google’s usage policy and use Web Risk where appropriate.

What Singapore and Malaysia scam patterns are included?
  • Romance-investment: fast rapport or future-talk followed by a private investment/crypto pitch.
  • Job/task: easy commissions or ratings paired with a request to pay/top up to continue or withdraw.
  • Impersonation: a claimed bank, police, government, or courier identity used with fear, secrecy, or urgency.
  • Parcel/fee: an unexpected delivery, customs, or release fee used to prompt payment or disclose details.
  • Mule recruitment: commission offered for receiving or moving money through a personal account.

These are cues for review, not proof. Local slang, nationality, and named agencies are not scam indicators by themselves.

Are the model’s scam labels verified?

No. Stages, pressure scores, notes, and extracted artifacts are model-generated research signals, not independently verified facts. A link scan match is a warning; a “no known threat” result is not a safety guarantee. Review context before drawing conclusions.

How to read these demo metrics
  • Chats are included when their latest stored message is within the last 30 days.
  • Pressure is the mean model assessment across bot replies in those chats: 1 low, 2 medium, 3 high.
  • Scam categories are grouped from each chat’s latest model-assessed stage. They are indicative labels, not independently verified scam classifications. Mean pressure is averaged across bot replies for chats in that category; the row shows both chat and assessment counts.
  • Message counts include each active chat's full stored history. Conversation span is the time from its first to last stored message, grouped by assigned persona.

These are stored demo/test conversations, not estimates of real-world scam prevalence or model accuracy.

PhishLab Terms of Use

PhishLab is a research and awareness prototype. Please review and accept these conditions before using the application.

Authorized, responsible use

Use PhishLab only for lawful scam-awareness, education, testing, or research that you are authorized to conduct. Do not use it to deceive, harass, impersonate, or target real people.

Use fictional or anonymized data

Do not enter passwords, one-time codes, financial details, confidential information, or personal data that you do not have permission to process.

AI output requires review

Replies, labels, scores, and observations may be incomplete or wrong. They are research signals—not verified scam verdicts, professional advice, or a substitute for human judgment.

External processing may occur

Conversation context may be sent to the configured model provider. A link is sent to Google Safe Browsing only when you explicitly request a link check.

Data and service conditions

  • Conversation messages and research observations are stored in the application’s configured database and may be available through its export tools.
  • You are responsible for obtaining any required consent and following applicable privacy, security, and data-protection requirements.
  • The prototype is provided for evaluation without guarantees of availability, accuracy, completeness, or fitness for a particular purpose.
  • Stop using the application if you do not understand or agree to these conditions.
